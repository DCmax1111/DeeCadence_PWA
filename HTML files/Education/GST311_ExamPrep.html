Chapter 1: Understanding Peace, Conflict, Security & Conflict Resolution
Most content-dense chapter — lots of MCQ fodder

Core Definitions

TermDefinition / Key Points
ConflictSituation of disagreement, disharmony, disaffection between individuals/groups. From Latin configere = confrontation. Involves incompatible goals. Can be intrapersonal or interpersonal.
PeaceFreedom from disturbance; tranquility and harmony. A kinetic phenomenon — always in transit, not a static destination. Peace without security is incomplete.
Conflict ManagementHandling a dispute to minimize negative outcomes. Does NOT necessarily end conflict. Involves limitation, containment, litigation. (Best, 2006)
Conflict ResolutionCreating a situation of peace — actually ending the conflict. After resolution comes conflict transformation (change in perception and attitude).
SecuritySafety; protection against harm, threats, risks. Safeguarding lives, property, and information.
⚠ Key distinction: Conflict Management ≠ Conflict Resolution. Management reduces/contains; Resolution ends it. Conflict Transformation comes after resolution.

Organisational Causes of Conflict

High Focus6 Organisational Perspective Causes
  • Competition over scarce resources — money, space, equipment, personnel
  • Ambiguity in responsibility and jurisdiction — confusion over who does what
  • Results of interdependence — performance depends on others; delays trigger conflict
  • Reward System — unequal salaries/bonuses create resentment
  • Differentiation — different sections view each other with suspicion
  • Competition over scarce resources (repeated for distribution) — self-serving bias

Interpersonal Causes of Conflict

  • Grudges — anger and ridicule leading to revenge
  • Wrong perception / faulty attribution — attributing bad motives to others' actions
  • Faulty communication — message sent ≠ message received

High Focus6 Conditions

  • Identification and Assessment — recognize existence, identify causes & parties
  • Mutual Respect — treat parties with respect even in dispute
  • Understanding the Cause — investigate root cause
  • Finding Common Grounds — identify shared interests
Traditional communication methods include: floral and symbolography (eyei, okono, kola nuts). Communication avenues: negotiation, mediation, inquiry, arbitration, conciliation.

Chapter 7: The Role of Language in Conflict Resolution
Language as catalyst & resolver; negotiation types; hate speech

Language Definitions

  • Language = ordered system of linguistic symbols used for communication
  • It mediates between self and society; is a vehicle of culture transmission
  • Words carry meaning that is linguistically, culturally, and contextually bound

Language as a Conflict Escalator

High Focus2 Key Forms
  • Competitive Language — justifications, personal attacks, excessive demands, threats. Motivated by "pro-self orientation" (Cordonnier). Focus on maximising personal outcome at expense of others.
  • Hate Speech — inflammatory vocabulary creating tension between groups. Example: during 1994 Rwandan Genocide, radio broadcasts called Tutsi "cockroaches".
FGN formulated a "Hate Speech" policy in response to conflict-triggering language in Nigeria.

Causes of Conflict from Language

High Focus
  • Language marginalisation — imposing a dominant language (e.g., Sri Lankan Tamil-Sinhalese conflict; the "Sinhala Only Act" disadvantaged Tamil speakers)
  • Variations in language semantics — same word different meanings in different cultures (Cohen, 2001)

Types of Negotiation (Ch. 7)

High FocusShedrack (2004): 2 Types
TypeCharacteristics
Positional NegotiationAggressive pursuit of interests; adversarial & competitive; breaks down easily
Collaborative NegotiationBoth parties discuss needs amicably; non-violent; no third parties normally
Example: South Africa Vice-President visited Nigeria for dialogue after xenophobia attacks — collaborative negotiation in action.

Mediation and Arbitration Language

  • Mediators must choose neutral, inclusive, respectful, non-threatening words
  • Language framing matters: "peace agreement" sounds better than "surrender"
  • Non-verbal cues are culture-bound — mediators must know cultural values

Language in Conflict Resolution

Common SenseHow Language Resolves Conflict
  • Exposes disharmony — word choice reveals acrimony; enables proactive action
  • Negotiation — direct back-and-forth dialogue toward agreement
  • Mediation/Arbitration — third-party language facilitation
  • Inclusive discourse — shared proverbs, religious texts, national metaphors; Truth and Reconciliation Commissions
  • Peace journalism — media language that supports healing, not wounds

Presidential Amnesty Programme

High FocusKey Details
  • Proclaimed: June 25, 2009 by President Umaru Musa Yar'Adua
  • Conditions: surrender weapons, renounce militancy, sign undertaking
  • Government pledged: DDR support + education scholarships + vocational training
  • ~25,000 ex-militants surrendered weapons
  • Initially designed to run for 5 years (but still running — creates "sense of entitlement")
  • Nigeria lost over 3 trillion Naira during peak crisis (Ezendu et al., 2013)

Amnesty Programme Objectives

  • Promotion of peace and stability
  • Rehabilitation and re-integration of ex-militants
  • Economic empowerment; reducing poverty and unemployment
  • Human capital development (education, vocational training)
  • Addressing root causes of militancy
  • Facilitating social re-integration and community acceptance
  • Contribution to sustainable development of the region

Challenges of the Amnesty Programme

High Focus
  • Corruption and mismanagement of funds
  • May encourage criminality (reward for militancy)
  • Could become conduit for siphoning resources
  • Raised demands from criminal groups in other regions
  • Lacks clear exit strategy (still running beyond 5 years)
  • Sustainability threatened by government funding reliance
  • Lacks long-term stability framework
  • Emboldened ex-militants as political actors manipulating funds
  • Continued violence from new emerging groups
  • Root causes (degradation, poverty, unemployment) not fully addressed
Chapter 9: Legal Perspective of Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR)
Most technical chapter — 9 ADR types with pros and cons
⚠ This chapter is the highest-risk for MCQs. You must distinguish between all 9 ADR types, their key features, advantages, and disadvantages.

What is ADR?

  • Alternative to court litigation for resolving disputes
  • Purpose: resolve disputes without litigation's disadvantages
  • Legal basis: Arbitration and Conciliation Act Cap. 19, Laws of the Federation 1990
  • Also applies the New York Convention on Recognition and Enforcement of Arbitral Awards

9 Types of ADR

High FocusAll 9 Types
N-M-A-C-M-N-M-P-M
Negotiation · Mediation · Arbitration · Conciliation · Mini Trial · Neutral Evaluation · Med-Arb · Private Judging · Multi-Door Court

Negotiation

  • Problem-solving process: two or more parties voluntarily discuss differences
  • Parties determine their own negotiator (unlike litigation where judge is assigned)
  • No third party involved normally
  • Exchanges can be tangible (money) or intangible (apology, attitude change)
AdvantagesDisadvantages
Easier and faster than litigationConfers legitimacy on the parties' demands
Informal; parties fully participateParties may appear weak
Parties choose their negotiatorDiscussions may be premature
Helps test strength of other partyMay give false hope or increase dispute visibility

Mediation

High Focus
  • Neutral third party (mediator) facilitates structured discussion
  • Mediator does NOT impose a decision — parties reach own agreement
  • Confidential process; discussions are "wholly without prejudice"
  • 4 stages: Information Session → Information Exchange → Negotiation → Agreement
AdvantagesDisadvantages
Less expensive than litigationEmployer participation may erode authority
Confidential procedureMay imply relief even without merit
Flexible; parties control outcomeNot all mediators are skilled
Interest-based (not just legal)Parties do not have to agree
Preserves relationshipsConfidentiality not guaranteed

Arbitration

High Focus
  • Neutral third party (arbitrator) renders a binding decision after hearing both sides
  • Legal definition (Kano State Urban Development Board v. Fanz Construction Co. Ltd, 1990): reference to a person other than a court of competent jurisdiction
  • Arbitration law roots: English Common Law, Nigerian Laws, Doctrine of Equity
  • Voluntary: parties mutually consent to select arbitrator
  • Compulsory: consent enforced by statutory provision
  • Cannot include constitutional or customary law matters — purely business/commercial disputes
  • Every arbitration agreement must be in writing
  • Arbitral award = final; binding and enforceable (Section 5(1))
AdvantagesDisadvantages
Reduces litigation costsArbitrator makes final decision — hard to appeal
Expedites resolutionMay "split the difference" unfairly
Reduces enmity vs. courtHearing may produce record used elsewhere
Limited judicial reviewAdversarial in nature
Arbitrators are less emotional than jurorsChallenges of due process

Conciliation

High FocusKey Distinction from Mediation and Arbitration
  • Neutral conciliator meets parties separately (unlike mediation, parties rarely face each other)
  • Conciliator has NO legal standing, no authority to seek evidence or call witnesses
  • Conciliator writes no decision and makes no award
  • Goal: seek concessions from parties (main difference from mediation)
  • Lowers tensions, improves communication, explores solutions
⚠ Conciliation ≠ Mediation: In mediation, mediator guides discussion to minimise parties' needs and reframes. In conciliation, the goal is to conciliate by seeking concessions. Parties rarely face each other in conciliation.

Mini Trial

  • Not a trial per se; also called "information exchange"
  • Resembles an abridged court case but without live evidence
  • A form of evaluative mediation
  • Uses a neutral third party called Advisor
  • If parties agree → Advisor draws up binding agreement; if deadlock → non-binding opinion
  • Proceedings are confidential but NOT binding on the parties

Neutral Evaluation

  • Parties present their case to a Neutral Evaluator (usually a retired judge or expert)
  • Evaluator provides strengths and weaknesses of each party's case
  • Helps facilitate access to mediation based on neutral analysis
  • Advisive and persuasive in nature (not binding)

Med-Arb

High Focus
  • Abbreviation of Mediation-Arbitration
  • First attempt: resolve through mediation
  • If mediation fails → proceed to arbitration (binding decision)
  • Same neutral third party serves as both mediator and then arbitrator
  • Agreement to go to arbitration if mediation fails is made before mediation starts

Private Judging

  • Parties hire a Private Judge to try their dispute
  • Where applicable law supports it, the decision is decisive and binding
  • Parties may define their own procedures (time, place)

Multi-Door Court (MDC)

High Focus
  • "Multiple doors" = various ADR options available at a court house
  • Available doors: Early Neutral Evaluation, Mediation, Arbitration
  • Court-annexed programme — "court" does NOT mean litigation!

MDC Objectives:

  • Provide enhanced, timely, cost-effective access to justice
  • Supplement avenues for justice with additional doors
  • Develop "Managerial Judges" concept
  • Utilise retired judges through mediation and arbitration services

MDC Process — 3 Stages:

  • Stage 1: Fill Request Form (Form 1) + Statement of Issues; Notice of Referral sent within 7 days; Responding party returns Submission Form within 7 days
  • Stage 2: Intake Screening + pre-session meeting with Dispute Resolution Specialist (DRS); parties fill Confirmation of Attendance Form (Form 4) + Confidentiality Agreement (Form 5); neutral's bio-data provided
  • Stage 3: ADR Session scheduled; Mediator/Arbitrator notified + Disclosure Form; session convened

Chapter 12: Mediation, Negotiation & Dialogue as Conflict Resolution Tools
3 mediation styles; 2 negotiation types; dialogue process; organisational conflict types

3 Styles of Mediation (Harvard Law School, 2024)

High FocusMemorise All 3
StyleKey Features
Facilitative MediationMediator = process guide only; does NOT impose solutions or judgements; focuses on improving communication; parties generate their own solutions; most widely recognised form
Evaluative MediationMediator takes a directive role; provides assessment of each party's strengths/weaknesses; proposes settlement options; predicts court outcomes; useful in legal/commercial disputes; may reduce party autonomy
Transformative MediationDeepest, relationship-centred approach; seeks to transform how parties interact and perceive each other; principles: empowerment + recognition; views conflict as opportunity for moral/relational growth
⚠ Key distinction: Facilitative = mediator guides process only. Evaluative = mediator gives expert opinions/assessments. Transformative = mediator aims to change the relationship itself.

  • Active listening — attentively listen; reflect and clarify
  • Reframing — restating message in neutral/positive way to reduce defensiveness
  • Building trust — maintaining impartiality, confidentiality, safe environment

High FocusDistributive vs. Integrative
TypeNatureOutcome
Distributive NegotiationWin-lose; fixed pie; competitive; one party gains at other's expenseZero-sum; relationship often damaged
Integrative NegotiationWin-win; collaborative; focuses on interests not positions; seeks mutual gainsValue creation; relationship preserved
Key Concept (Fisher, Ury & Patton, 2011): Positions = explicit demands. Interests = underlying needs/desires that motivate those positions. Always focus on interests, not positions.
